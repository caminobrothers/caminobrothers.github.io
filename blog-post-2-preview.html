How Many Kilometres Should I Walk Per Day on the Camino?

By Mike · 6 min read · Notes from the Way

  • TopicDistance
  • Our averageabout 23 km a day
  • Our range9 to 39 km
  • Walking days counted76

Short answer: for most pilgrims, 20 to 25 km a day (about 12 to 16 miles) is the honest working range. Fit, experienced walkers settle at 25 to 30. New walkers should start at 15 to 20 and let the number grow. But treat those as starting points, not targets - because on a real Camino, the distance is rarely yours to choose. The towns choose it.

Here is what our own record says. Across seven route caminos - Spain, Portugal, Germany and Austria - we have logged 76 walking days with the distances written down. The shortest was 8.9 km. The longest was 39. The average is about 23 km a day. Almost everything we know about daily distance is inside that spread.

The towns decide more than you do

A Camino day does not end at a kilometre mark. It ends at a bed, in a town, and the towns are where they are. If the next good stop is 24 km away, your day is 24 km - or 31, if you push on to the one after. That is how our biggest days happened:

  • 39 km, O Cebreiro down to Sarria in 2021 - the stages simply stacked up that way, and Sarria was the stop we wanted
  • 36 km, Leon to Hospital de Orbigo, also 2021 - on our very first day of that camino, which is not a method we recommend
  • 34 km days turned up in Portugal, on the Rhine and on the way to Finisterre - never because we chased a number, always because that was where the day ended

And it is also how our shortest day happened: 8.9 km from Koblenz to Vallendar in 2022, because that was the day's stage and there was no reason to invent more. Nobody's Camino was ruined by a 9 km day. A few have been ruined by a 39.

Long day, short day

Nicki plans our German and Austrian routes on a pattern he stated outright in our chat: a long day of 20 to 25 km, followed by a short day under 15. Look at our Jakobsweg in 2024 and the pattern is right there: 24, 13, 18, 18, 25, 13, 14, 21, 30. The short days are not lost days. They are when feet recover, laundry gets done, and you arrive somewhere in time to actually see it.

The same rhythm shows up even when we did not plan it. Our 34 km day on the Rhine in 2022 was followed by 22, then 12.4. The 39 km into Sarria was the end of that stretch of walking altogether. Your body will impose the pattern if you do not - better to plan it than to be taught it.

Terrain is the other half of the number

25 km is not one thing. On the flat floor of the Inn valley in Austria, or across the meseta in Spain, 25 km is a steady day. In the mountains - climbing to O Cebreiro, or a Tyrol day with a few hundred metres of climbing in it - 20 km can be the bigger day. When you plan a stage, look at the climbing as well as the distance. A short day with a mountain in the middle of it is not a short day.

Time makes the same point. Walking pace with a pack is roughly 4 km an hour, and a real day includes a coffee stop, a long lunch with a couple of beers if you are doing it right, and a few pauses nobody logs. So:

  • 15 km is about 4 to 5 hours, door to door
  • 20 km is about 5.5 to 6.5 hours
  • 25 km is about 7 to 8 hours
  • 30 km and over is 8 to 9 hours, and the day starts to be about nothing else

Leave in the morning with those hours in mind and you will arrive in daylight, in time for a shower, a wash of clothes and a table. Arrive at dusk every day and the Camino becomes a commute.

Rest days are part of the distance

On our first camino we took a full rest day at Caldas de Reis, a spa town in Galicia, and walked on the next morning with new legs. On the second, our rest day landed in Finisterre at the end, with the ocean doing the recovering for us. A rest day does not lower your average by much - but it resets everything the average is costing you. Our working rule: one rest day, or at least one very short day, in every week of walking. If your daily number only works with no rest days in it, the number is too big.

Finding your number

  • Start from the route, not your fitness. Divide the kilometres you want to walk by the walking days you honestly have (that is post 1's maths). If the answer comes out above 30, the plan - not your legs - needs to change
  • Test it at home, on back-to-back days. One long walk proves almost nothing. Walk your planned distance two days running, with your pack. The second morning is the truth
  • Start the camino shorter than your number. Give the first two or three days 15 to 20 km while your feet and shoulders learn the job. We do not always take our own advice - see the 36 km first day above - but we give it
  • Let the number breathe. Plan a range, not a figure: a long day when the towns stretch out and the ground is flat, a short one after it, a rest day where the map offers something worth resting in. The walkers who finish are not the ones with the biggest daily number. They are the ones whose number bent instead of breaking them

The honest summary

There is no correct daily distance, and the pilgrims walking beside you are doing everything from 10 to 40 km. Our own 76 days averaged about 23, and the shape of the thing matters more than the average: long and short alternating, terrain counted, a rest day in the week, and every day ending at a bed in a town that was chosen, not just reached. Plan the rhythm and the kilometres look after themselves.

Buen camino - at whatever distance is yours.
